Welcome

Recovery is where training becomes capacity

A workout is the signal to change. Much of the change itself happens in the hours and days that follow, as your body recovers. This guide explains how recovery works, what matters most, and how to tell when you need more of it.

It’s for anyone who trains: lifting, running, playing a sport, working with us in Functional Strength, or doing physically demanding work. You don’t need to be an athlete. Recovery matters just as much when the goal is carrying groceries, keeping up with your kids or hiking without paying for it all week.

Recovery isn’t only physical, either. Training, work, family and stress all draw on the same reserves.1 A hard week at work is a hard week for your body too.

At TAB we call this Balance: matching challenge with recovery, so progress is something you can sustain.

How it works

How training turns into capacity

Training asks your body for a little more than it’s used to. You come away temporarily tired, then recover, and adapt to be a little more capable than before.3

Timing is everything

The same training can build you up or wear you down, depending on how much recovery sits between sessions.

Too little recovery

Each session starts before you’ve bounced back, so fatigue piles up and progress stalls or slips.3

About right

Each session lands once you’ve recovered, so small gains add up over weeks and months.

Too long a gap

Sessions are so far apart that the effect fades before the next one, and you stay roughly where you are.

A simple model, not a formula: how fast you recover depends on the session, your sleep, food, age, stress and more, and it varies from person to person and week to week.1

Transform. Adapt. Balance. is how we work. In training, Balance means progress you can keep making, rather than a burst of effort followed by burnout or injury.

The foundations

Start with the basics

Recovery has a pecking order. Get the foundations right and the extras become optional; skip them and no extra will make up for it.

  1. Extras

    Massage, compression, foam rolling, cold. Helpful for some things, in the right place (page 7).

  2. A sensible plan

    Rest days, easier days and gradual progress, so recovery has room to happen (page 6).

  3. Food and fluids

    Enough to eat, enough protein, and fluids through the day and around training (page 5).

  4. Sleep

    The foundation. Regular, sufficient sleep supports everything above it.

How much sleep?

The CDC recommends:2

AgeSleep a night

65 and older7 to 8 hours

Habits that help

  • Go to bed and get up at the same time every day.
  • Keep your bedroom quiet, relaxing and cool.
  • Turn off screens at least 30 minutes before bed.
  • Avoid large meals and alcohol before bed, and caffeine in the afternoon or evening.
  • Keep exercising regularly.2

Struggling to sleep for weeks, snoring loudly or waking unrefreshed? Talk to your healthcare provider.

Fuel and fluids

Fuel for recovery

Recovery needs raw materials. You don’t need a perfect diet; you need enough, often enough.

Protein: enough, spread through the day

For most people who exercise, the International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight a day, in servings of about 20 to 40 g, spread every 3 to 4 hours.4 For someone who weighs 70 kg (154 lb), that’s roughly 100 to 140 g a day.

Before or after training both work: the effect of a session lasts at least 24 hours.4

Over 65? Protein matters more, not less

An expert group on older adults advises at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg a day, and 1.2 g or more for people who exercise or are otherwise active.5 People with severe kidney disease may need to limit protein instead, so talk to your doctor first.5

Carbohydrate for long or hard sessions

Carbohydrate refuels working muscles. It matters most for endurance training, long sessions, or training twice in a day.4 Skimping on it is one of the things doctors check when fatigue won’t lift.3

Fluids, and eating enough overall

Drink through the day and around training, and more when it’s hot or you’ve sweated a lot. Eating too little for the training you do undermines recovery; it’s another of the causes doctors look for when someone stays run down.3

Food first. Whole foods can meet most people’s protein needs; supplements are a convenience, not a requirement.4 Turning these ranges into meals that fit your week is what Integrated Nutrition at TAB is for. It’s coaching, not medical nutrition therapy: if you have a medical condition, your physician or a registered dietitian sets your diet.

Planning

Plan for recovery, not just training

The best plan builds recovery in, so it isn’t left to chance.

Rest days are part of the plan

U.S. guidelines advise muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week, plus 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity such as brisk walking.6 Well spread, that leaves plenty of room for easier days.

Build up gradually

Introduce new or harder exercises over a week or two rather than all at once. Soreness is most common when you’re starting out, returning after a break, or trying something new.7

After a very sore session

Train lighter and shorter for a day or two, or work the parts of your body that aren’t sore.7 Soreness usually peaks a day or two after a session and fades within about four days.8

Count the rest of life

Work, family and stress draw on the same reserves as training.1 On a heavy week, an easier session is often the smarter one.

Signs you need more recovery

  • Fatigue that hangs on, even after easier days
  • Performance that drops and stays down
  • Mood changes: flat, irritable or unmotivated3
  • Picking up more illnesses or niggling injuries1

Back off for a while and look at the foundations: sleep, food and the rest of your life. If it doesn’t lift, see your doctor. Persistent fatigue can have medical causes, such as low iron, that are worth ruling out.3

Get medical help right away

if, after hard exercise, you have muscle pain far worse than you’d expect, weakness, or dark urine. These can be signs of rhabdomyolysis, a serious muscle injury.9

The honest picture

Recovery tools: what the evidence says

Recovery is a big market. Some tools help with some things; none replaces the foundations.

ToolWhat the research suggestsOur take
MassageIn a 2018 review of recovery methods, massage was the most effective for next-day soreness and the feeling of fatigue.10Worth it when it fits your training and budget. Timing matters (page 8).
Compression garmentsCan help with the feeling of fatigue.10Reasonable if you like them.
Cold-water immersionCan ease the feeling of fatigue.10 But after every strength session for 12 weeks, it led to smaller gains in strength and muscle than easy active recovery.11Not after every strength session. Better kept for when you need to bounce back fast between events.
Foam rollingEffects on recovery are minor; it may ease muscle pain a little and suits a warm-up better than recovery.12Fine as part of a warm-up. Don’t expect much more.
StretchingDoesn’t meaningfully reduce soreness, before or after exercise.13Stretch for mobility, not to prevent soreness.
Easy movementExercise is the most effective way to ease soreness, though the relief is temporary.7A walk or an easy spin on sore days.
SupplementsWhole foods can meet protein needs; supplements are a convenience.4Food first.

“Our take” is TAB’s reading of the evidence for everyday training, not a rule. If something helps you and does no harm, it may be worth keeping.

Myths and facts

Common myths, and what’s true

Recovery advice travels fast. Here are the ideas we hear most, and what the evidence says.

You may have heardWhat’s true
“No pain, no gain.”Soreness isn’t a scorecard.It’s most common when an exercise is new or you’re returning after a break,7 so it isn’t a reliable measure of a good session.
“Rest days lose progress.”Rest days are when progress happens.Hard training without enough recovery leads to stalled or falling performance, not faster gains.3
“Stretching prevents soreness.”It doesn’t, meaningfully.Stretching before or after exercise doesn’t produce a worthwhile drop in soreness.13 Stretch for mobility instead.
“An ice bath after every workout speeds up gains.”It may do the opposite.Used after every strength session, cold-water immersion led to smaller gains in strength and muscle.11
“You need supplements to recover.”Food comes first.Whole foods can meet most people’s protein needs; supplements are a convenience.4
“Recovery is just about the body.”Life counts too.Work, family and stress draw on the same reserves as training.1
